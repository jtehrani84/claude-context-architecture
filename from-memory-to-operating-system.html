The MechanismHow Corrections Become Autonomy

Nobody planned a 2.1 GB local intelligence system. It grew out of three forces that keep compounding on each other.

1 — Friction became architecture

Every mistake Claude made, I corrected, and each correction turned into a memory file. When the same mistake kept coming back it became a rule, and a rule Claude ignored became a hook. The hooks that needed real data pulled in MCP servers, and when those servers overflowed the context window, context-mode came in to absorb them. Each layer exists because the one before it wasn't enough on its own.

2 — The non-developer advantage

I never wrote a line of Python, and I never authored a YAML skill file or debugged a hook script either. Every piece of infrastructure (the hooks, the graph indexer, the skills) was built by Claude from my requirements. The barrier was never coding ability; it's knowing what you want and being specific about the constraints.

3 — Compound learning is exponential

The first twenty days were memory and the wiki — the filing system. Days 20 to 40 layered on hooks and MCP servers for enforcement, and the stretch from day 40 to 60 added skills, the graph, and context-mode as an expertise layer. Each layer made the next one faster to build, so by day 60 Claude could stand up a new skill in about 5 minutes, because it already had the patterns from the previous 179.

The ShiftFrom Passive to Active

Part 1 described a passive system that stored things and loaded them when they were relevant. What grew out of it is active — it intercepts, validates, and enforces without a human in the loop.

Part 1 (Passive)Now (Active)
"Hooks auto-suggest wiki pages" → Hooks block unsafe operations, validate output, enrich graphs
"Memory persists corrections" → Memory is entity-linked, graph-indexed, auto-curated
"Rules load as standards" → Rules enforced by hook chains — gated, not suggested
"Wiki pages load on demand" → Wiki has inbox, lint, auto-capture, entity extraction
"CLAUDE.md routes context" → CLAUDE.md is a full orchestration manifest
"Contacts live in CRM" → Entity pages capture working style, decision patterns, relationship history

The pattern

Every passive component attracted an active counterpart — storage pulled in enforcement, reference pulled in validation, memory pulled in curation. The system developed an immune response to its own failure modes.

Entity PagesThe Relationship Layer

Memory stores corrections and the wiki stores knowledge, but neither one stores people. Entity pages close that gap with a structured page per person that compounds across every interaction.

There are two templates, internal and external. The structured top gives you instant prep (role, org position, working style, territory), and an append-only timeline below it grows with every meeting, decision, and collaboration. The pages are seeded programmatically from Slack profiles, then enriched by overnight intelligence crons.

WithoutWith entity pages
"Who's the AE on that account?" → Full org tree with SE/AE assignments in one Read
"What did we discuss last time?" → Timeline shows every interaction chronologically
Meeting prep = 20 min searching → One page, auto-routed by SessionStart hook

So far that's 150+ people mapped across the sales and solutions org trees, every leader sitting there with their reports, Slack IDs, and coverage areas. The CRM knows the accounts; entity pages know the humans working them.

InteractionsWhere the Real Payoff Lives

Individual layers are useful on their own, but the interactions between them are where compound growth actually happens.

Loop 1 — Error → Memory → Guardrail → Prevention

Deploy with --set-env-vars instead of --update-env-vars and every env var gets wiped. You correct Claude, the correction lands in memory, and that memory gets cited in an infrastructure rule — so now a hook blocks any deploy that uses --set-env-vars, and that whole error class can't happen again, even in a brand new session.

Loop 2 — Research → Wiki → Graph → Routing

You research Agentforce Agent Script, Claude writes it up as a wiki page, and the graph extracts the entities from it. Next session, when you mention "agent routing," the SessionStart hook surfaces that wiki page along with related memories and the architecture doc — the context arrives before you ask for it.

Loop 3 — Skill → MCP → Context-Mode → Output

Run /account-prep and the skill already knows the workflow. It calls the MCP servers for CRM, news, and web, each response compresses through context-mode, and then the skill assembles the prep doc using the anti-slop rules and corporate grounding. That's one command driving five systems with zero manual context management.

This is the compound effect, and no single layer produces it on its own. Memory alone doesn't prevent errors, hooks alone don't know what's dangerous, and skills alone can't reach external data. The value lives in the connections between them.

LessonAgents Forget to Remember — at Every Layer

In Part 1 I cited DDR-001 from the Agentforce Agent Harness and its line "agents forget to remember," and the fix at the time was intrinsic constraint awareness. Two months on, the same DDR-001 shows up at every layer of the stack.

Layer"Forgetting" Failure ModeFix
MemoryDoesn't check before claimingGraph recalls relevant memories proactively
RulesReads but ignores under pressurePreToolUse hook intercepts before execution
SkillsReinvents instead of loadingSkill routing matches task automatically
WikiPattern-matches from training dataADR hook injects decisions when files in scope
MCPHallucinates instead of queryingGrounding rules require real data queries

The meta-lesson is that every knowledge source needs a matching enforcement mechanism, because knowledge without enforcement is only a suggestion, while knowledge with enforcement becomes a constraint. Constraints compound over time; suggestions quietly decay.

LessonsWhat 60 Days Taught Me

1 — The system builds itself

I didn't plan most of this — each correction, wiki page, and skill grew out of a specific session's need, and the five-tier framework just gave those decisions somewhere to land.

2 — Enforcement matters more than knowledge

128 memory files didn't stop my infrastructure mistakes, but three PreToolUse hooks did. If you build only one new thing after Part 1, make it a guardrail hook for your most expensive mistake.

3 — Context overflow is the real scaling limit

Part 1 optimized startup, cutting it from 15K to 5K tokens, but the real limit hit at minute 45, when research filled the window. Context-mode was the breakthrough that made multi-hour sessions possible.

4 — Skills are the highest-return investment

One skill replaces about 30 minutes of manual instruction per session. I've got 186 of them, assembled from three suites (project management, Salesforce platform, and developer tooling), plus 28 custom commands I directed Claude to build for my specific SE workflow. You don't write 186 skills from scratch — you install the right systems and customize the last mile.

5 — You don't need to be a developer

I've never opened a Python file to write the hooks, and I've never hand-built a skill. Claude builds its own infrastructure from my direction. It comes down to domain expertise, clear requirements, and an AI that builds its own tooling.

The Kit (5 layers, 45 minutes to deploy)

Layer 1 — Identity Skeleton

Template CLAUDE.md with the routing-table structure, role definition, project registry, and essential standards baked in (anti-slop, anti-hallucination), plus a pre-wired wiki index. You fill in YOUR role, YOUR projects, and YOUR constraints, which takes about 10 minutes.

Layer 2 — Rules & Guardrails

Three starter rules files — communication.md (voice standards, banned patterns), security-governance.md (CRUD/FLS, sharing, secrets), and architecture.md (decision framework, preferred patterns). Then two hook scripts — session-init for context routing on start, and one PreToolUse guardrail that blocks your most expensive recurring mistake.

Layer 3 — SE Skill Pack

Five production-ready skills — /account-prep (pre-meeting intelligence), /deal-strategy (competitive + talk track), /email-draft (anti-slop customer email), /post-meeting (capture + CRM + follow-up), and /demo-prep (script from brief). Each one replaces about 30 minutes of manual instruction per use.

Layer 4 — Intelligence Automation

A Slack channel roster with 25 pre-mapped internal channels (product, competitive, enablement, win/loss, leadership), plus overnight gather scripts that scan Exa, HN, GitHub, X, and Slack and synthesize a morning brief. There's one MCP server (GitHub) in there to prove the action layer works. Swap in your own OU channels and go.

Layer 5 — Entity Pages

A wiki/people/ directory with two templates, internal and external. You seed your org tree from Slack profiles (Claude pulls name, title, email, and timezone programmatically), and each page puts org position at the top with an append-only timeline below it. After one session, every leader and their reports are mapped; after a month, you've got a relationship graph no CRM captures.
